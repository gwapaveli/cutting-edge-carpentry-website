Best Wood for Decks in Ontario

The short answer

Pressure-Treated Frame, Then Choose Your Decking

For most decks in Southwestern Ontario, the frame underneath is pressure-treated lumber, whatever goes on top. The real choice is the decking and railings: pressure-treated, cedar or composite.

Pressure-treated costs the least up front. Cedar looks and feels better but costs more and is softer. Composite costs the most but needs the least upkeep. There's no single best option: the right one depends on your budget, how much maintenance you're willing to do, how much sun and weather the deck gets, and how long you plan to stay in the home.

  • Lowest cost: pressure-treated decking
  • Best natural look: cedar
  • Least maintenance: composite or PVC
  • Always: a well-built pressure-treated frame on proper footings

Start with the frame

What Holds the Deck Up Matters Most

The posts, beams and joists carry the deck, and in most Ontario decks they're pressure-treated lumber. Treated lumber is graded for where it's used: posts and anything in or near the ground need a rating for ground contact, while joists and beams above ground can use an above-ground rating. Ask what's being used, because the wrong grade near the soil is a common reason deck framing rots early.

Footings matter just as much. Ontario's freeze-thaw cycles can heave footings that aren't deep enough, so they're typically set below the frost line, commonly about 4 feet (1.2 m) in this part of the province, or an engineered alternative like helical piles is used. Your municipality's building department sets the requirements for permitted decks.

Decking options

Comparing Deck Materials

How the four main decking choices compare on cost, upkeep and feel.

Pressure-treated lumber

The most common and least expensive choice. It's strong, widely available and takes stain well once it has dried. It also moves more than other options, so expect some checking, cupping and greying unless it's sealed and maintained every few years.

Cedar

Western red cedar has natural resistance to rot and insects, weighs less and has a warmer look. It's softer, so it dents and scratches more easily, and it costs more than pressure-treated. It needs a finish to keep its colour; left alone it weathers to grey.

Composite and PVC

Made from wood fibre and plastic (composite) or all plastic (PVC), these boards don't need staining and hold their colour. They cost more up front, can get hot in direct sun, and need the frame built to the manufacturer's joist spacing, which is often tighter than for wood.

Tropical hardwoods

Dense hardwoods like ipe are extremely durable and very hard, but expensive, heavy and slow to work with, since fasteners need pre-drilling. They're less common on residential decks in this area.

Our recommendation

What We Usually Suggest

For most families we work with, a pressure-treated frame with pressure-treated or cedar decking, finished with a quality penetrating stain, gives the best balance of cost and lifespan.

If you'd rather not stain every few years and the budget allows, composite decking on a pressure-treated frame is worth pricing out.

Whatever the decking, the details decide how long a deck lasts: proper footings, flashing where the deck meets the house, corrosion-resistant fasteners, and room for air to move underneath so the frame dries out.

For existing decks, our refinishing service cleans and re-coats the wood, with CUTEK® Extreme penetrating oil as our premium standard when the surface is a fit.

FAQ

Deck Material Questions

Quick answers to what homeowners ask us most about deck materials.

How long does a pressure-treated deck last in Ontario?

It depends heavily on how it's built and maintained. A well-built pressure-treated deck that's kept clean and re-coated regularly commonly lasts 15 years or more, and the frame often outlasts the deck boards when it can dry out between rains.

Should I stain a new pressure-treated deck right away?

Usually not. New treated lumber is often still wet from the treatment process. A common test is to sprinkle water on the boards: if it beads up, wait; if it soaks in, the wood is dry enough to stain. That can take anywhere from a few weeks to a few months.

Is composite decking worth the extra cost?

It can be, if you plan to stay in the home and don't want to stain every few years. Compare the total cost over 10 to 15 years, including stain and your time, not just the price of the boards.

Do I need a permit to build a deck?

Often, especially if the deck is raised, large or attached to the house. Requirements vary by municipality, so check with your local building department or ask us to confirm before work starts.
